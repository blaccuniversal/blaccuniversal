I went to my favourite spot for home-cooked meals recently. There’s this woman who makes some of the best local food I’ve had, and I was there to pick up my usual three-day supply of soups, and this time I had opted for Afang and bitter leaf soups.

I sat there, waiting, minding my business and calculating which financial miracle would get me through the rest of the year. Detty December? I know I’m having the cleanest one sha, because getting by November alone is looking like a tough workout, and each day feels like a stack of evil reps.

Then another customer walked in.

A woman in her thirties, judging by her diction, although her face looked like life had personally taken her through at least five presidential elections.

A few minutes later, I heard her say:

“I say no put meat, e dey make me fat.”

I looked up. She didn’t look particularly fat, so I briefly wondered if someone else had said it. Nobody had.

The salesgirl replied, “Madam, we get fish o.”

“I SAY NO PUT MEAT! E DEY MAKE ME FAT. I NO WAN FAT”

Now, I don’t know what her actual motivation was, but I had a suspicion that the conversation had very little to do with weight management and a lot to do with the state of the economy.

And just like that, my heart sank a little.

Since when did eating meat become a financial decision that required this much negotiation?

There are things that shouldn’t be luxuries. Food, electricity, decent roads, clean water and access to basic healthcare shouldn’t be items on a Nigerian’s wish list. They are the bare minimum of what a functioning country should provide.

But here we are, in 2026, having conversations about whether we can afford to put meat in our soup.

For perspective, Nigeria became independent in 1960. Singapore became independent in 1965. Mauritius followed in 1968, while Bangladesh gained independence in 1971.

Singapore, a country with virtually no natural resources to speak of, has built an economy that is the envy of many nations. Mauritius, a small island nation, has transformed its economy into one of Africa’s strongest. Bangladesh, despite its enormous population, limited land and vulnerability to natural disasters, has achieved near-universal electricity access.

According to 2024 World Bank data, electricity access stood at 62.5% in Nigeria, compared with 99.5% in Bangladesh and 100% in Mauritius and Singapore.

Let that sink in.

I’m using that analogy because some of us are quick to say “Nigeria is young, don’t compare it to America, or UK or Canada”. But Nigeria is basically Kuvukiland with the ego of those three aforementioned and that’s why sometimes, people compare it to them. But we get it, they are old. What of these newer countries? What’s the excuse?

Countries that became independent after us, with considerably different resource endowments and challenges of their own, have managed to make progress in areas where we are still having national conversations about basic survival.

And no, I’m not suggesting that these countries are paradise. They have their own problems. But surely, after more than six decades, we should have something better to show for our own journey.

Nigeria has oil, gas, fertile land, an enormous population, brilliant people and an abundance of entrepreneurial energy. What we seem to have in shorter supply is the ability to translate all these advantages into a decent quality of life for the average citizen.

And perhaps the most astonishing part is that some people are actively campaigning for the very political establishment they believe should be held accountable for these conditions. At this point, political loyalty is beginning to look like a subscription service with no visible benefits.

We have cars that can fly, artificial intelligence writing code, reusable rockets returning from space, and scientists exploring possibilities beyond our planet. Meanwhile, Nigerians are still budgeting around electricity, buying petrol to power generators, debating on how many watts of inverter and solar to install, and pizza, ice cream and shawarma are luxuries that are only had on special occasions for most that aren’t the one percenters.

We are not exactly short of miracles. We just seem to have misplaced the people responsible for delivering them.

And before someone says I’m being unnecessarily negative, I think there’s a difference between criticising your country and giving up on it. If anything, expecting better is an expression of hope.

What worries me is how easily we’ve normalised things that should outrage us. We’ve become so accustomed to managing hardship that we sometimes mistake survival for progress.

Even more concerning is that madness no longer announces itself by eating garbage or dressing in rags. Sometimes, it comes dressed in expensive clothes, speaks impeccable English, holds multiple degrees and has a very respectable social standing. Then it opens its mouth, and its madness staring at you.

Anyway, I collected my three-day supply of soup and went home.

I still have bills to pay, I dey find work and love and I absolutely have no plans for Detty December.

Did I put meat in my own soup? I no know sha. But e dey make me fat too.
